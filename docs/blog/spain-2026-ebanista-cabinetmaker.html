On 15 October 2026, Correos issued Oficios antiguos. Ebanista. (Ancient Trades: Cabinetmaker), a stamp dedicated to one of the most exacting of the traditional woodworking crafts. The ebanista is the artisan who turns a simple strip or board of timber into luxury objects and pieces of real artistic value — and the issue celebrates the patience, precision and sensitivity that the trade demands.

The Craft Behind the Stamp

Patience, precision and a taste for detail, together with a certain artistic sensitivity and a genuine passion for working with wood, are among the qualities the cabinetmaker's trade requires. The ebanista is a craftsman capable of transforming a strip or a plain board into luxury objects and pieces of great artistic value.

The trade has always sat at the meeting point of technique and art. A cabinetmaker does not simply cut and join timber; they read the material, choosing how each board will be used, how its grain will run, and how the finished piece will look and feel. That combination of practical skill and aesthetic judgement is exactly what this stamp sets out to honour.

A Name Born from Ebony

The Spanish word ebanista comes from ébano — ebony — the dark, hard and highly prized wood once used for fine furniture and luxury decorative objects. Over time the term came to describe artisans specialising in fine woodwork regardless of the species they used, though the material is always a noble wood such as walnut, mahogany or cherry.

Skills and Techniques

The cabinetmaker must pay close attention to proportion, form, colour and the natural grain of the wood. They need to know the techniques of carving, marquetry, turning and inlay — the decorative work that gives each piece its character. They must also master finishing methods such as varnishing, waxing and polishing, processes that protect the timber while bringing out its natural beauty.

Joints Without Metal Fittings

One of the most singular and emblematic techniques of cabinetmaking is the encastre — the fitting together of different pieces of wood so that, from apparently impossible angles, everything locks into place like a three-dimensional puzzle. Mortise-and-tenon joints, dovetails and mitre joints allow solid furniture to be built without metal hardware, while still achieving great strength and durability.

For the first time, the premium sheet is printed on wood paper, and the design has been drawn with a laser — a fitting technical first for a stamp about craftsmanship in wood.

From Furniture to Restoration

The cabinetmaker's field of work is remarkably broad. It ranges from the manufacture of structures, doors, windows and furniture to the restoration of those same pieces, as well as the creation of objects of great complexity and quality. Few traditional trades touch so many parts of daily life while remaining so closely tied to a single material.

Between Tradition and Innovation

Today cabinetmaking balances tradition against innovation. Computer-aided design, digital machines and modern tools make certain processes faster and easier, but they do not replace the meticulous work of the cabinetmaker — above all in jobs that require judgement, experience and sensitivity. The stamp captures that balance: an old craft, still evolving, still dependent on the hand and eye of the maker.

Why This Issue Matters

Oficios antiguos. Ebanista. is part of Correos' wider interest in the traditional trades of Spain — the crafts that shaped everyday objects long before mass production. For collectors of topical stamps it is a rich subject: woodworking, craftsmanship, design and the conservation of heritage all meet in a single issue. And with its wood-paper premium sheet and laser-drawn artwork, it is also a technically unusual stamp that rewards a close look.
